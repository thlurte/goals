### 🔹 Part 1: Advanced Theorems & Derivations (Thursday Syllabus)
* **Book**: *Introduction to Probability* — Dimitri P. Bertsekas & John N. Tsitsiklis
* **Sections**: §11.5–11.6 (*Random Walks on Graphs, Detailed Balance & Time Reversibility*)
* **Theorems & Mathematical Concepts**:
  1. **Detailed Balance Equations & Time Reversibility**:
     * A probability distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ satisfies detailed balance if:
       $$\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j p_{ji} \quad (\forall i, j \in S)$$
     * **Theorem**: Any distribution satisfying detailed balance is guaranteed to be stationary:
       $$\sum_{i \in S} \pi_i p_{ij} = \sum_{i \in S} \pi_j p_{ji} = \pi_j \sum_{i \in S} p_{ji} = \pi_j(1) = \pi_j \implies \boldsymbol{\pi} P = \boldsymbol{\pi}$$
  2. **Random Walks on Connected Undirected Graphs**:
     * Let $G = (V, E)$ be an undirected connected graph with degree $d_i = \operatorname{deg}(i)$ and total edges $|E| = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i \in V} d_i$.
     * Transition probability: $p_{ij} = \frac{1}{d_i}$ for $(i, j) \in E$.
     * **Stationary Distribution Theorem**:
       $$\pi_i = \frac{d_i}{2|E|}$$
       * **Proof**: $\pi_i p_{ij} = \frac{d_i}{2|E|} \cdot \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{2|E|} = \frac{d_j}{2|E|} \cdot \frac{1}{d_j} = \pi_j p_{ji}$.
* **Pencil & Paper Problem Set**:
  - [ ] **Proof 1**: Prove that if $\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j p_{ji}$ holds for all pairs $(i, j)$, then $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ is stationary ($\boldsymbol{\pi} P = \boldsymbol{\pi}$).
  - [ ] **Proof 2**: Prove that for a regular graph where all vertices have degree $d_i = k$, the stationary distribution is strictly uniform $\pi_i = \frac{1}{|V|}$.
  - [ ] **Problem 3**: On a 5-node star graph with central hub node 1 connected to 4 perimeter leaf nodes $\{2, 3, 4, 5\}$ ($d_1 = 4, d_2=d_3=d_4=d_5=1, |E|=4$):
    * Calculate $\boldsymbol{\pi} = \left[ \frac{4}{8}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{8} \right]$.
    * Compute the mean return time to the hub node $E[T_{11}] = \frac{1}{\pi_1} = 2.0\text{ steps}$.

### 🔹 Part 2: Core Proofs & Problem Sets (Friday Syllabus)
* **Book**: *Introduction to Probability* — Dimitri P. Bertsekas & John N. Tsitsiklis
* **Sections**: §12.1–12.3 (*Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) & Metropolis-Hastings Theory*)
* **Theorems & Mathematical Concepts**:
  1. **The MCMC Paradigm**:
     * Sampling from unnormalized target distribution $p(x) = \frac{\tilde{p}(x)}{Z}$ where normalizing constant $Z = \int \tilde{p}(x)dx$ is analytically intractable.
     * Construct an ergodic Markov chain whose unique stationary distribution is exactly $p(x)$.
  2. **Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm**:
     * Given current state $x$, generate candidate $y \sim q(y \mid x)$ from proposal distribution $q$.
     * Accept candidate with probability:
       $$\alpha(x, y) = \min\left( 1, \frac{p(y) q(x \mid y)}{p(x) q(y \mid x)} \right) = \min\left( 1, \frac{\tilde{p}(y) q(x \mid y)}{\tilde{p}(x) q(y \mid x)} \right)$$
     * If accepted, $X_{n+1} = y$; else $X_{n+1} = x$.
  3. **Detailed Balance Proof**:
     * For $x \ne y$, transition probability is $p(x, y) = q(y \mid x) \alpha(x, y)$.
     * $$p(x) p(x, y) = p(x) q(y \mid x) \min\left( 1, \frac{p(y) q(x \mid y)}{p(x) q(y \mid x)} \right) = \min\left( p(x) q(y \mid x), p(y) q(x \mid y) \right)$$
     * By symmetry of the $\min$ function: $p(x) p(x, y) = p(y) p(y, x)$, proving detailed balance!
* **Pencil & Paper Problem Set**:
  - [ ] **Proof 1**: Derive the detailed balance identity $p(x) p(x, y) = p(y) p(y, x)$ under both cases: Case A ($\frac{p(y) q(x \mid y)}{p(x) q(y \mid x)} \le 1$) and Case B ($> 1$).
  - [ ] **Proof 2**: Prove that for symmetric proposal distributions ($q(y \mid x) = q(x \mid y)$, e.g., Gaussian random walk), the acceptance ratio simplifies to standard Metropolis rule $\alpha(x, y) = \min\left(1, \frac{p(y)}{p(x)}\right)$.
  - [ ] **Problem 3**: In MCMC exploration of a high-dimensional vector sphere with target $p(\mathbf{x}) \propto \exp(-\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 / 2\sigma^2)$ using Gaussian proposal $\mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}, \sigma_p^2 \mathbf{I})$, derive the acceptance probability $\alpha(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \min\left(1, \exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{y}\|^2 - \|\mathbf{x}\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\right)$.
